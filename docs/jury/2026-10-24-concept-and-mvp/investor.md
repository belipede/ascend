## Juror: Investor
Recommendation: CONDITIONAL. Support the personal MVP. Do not support any commercial investment until demand is validated.

Strongest argument:
- The cost of the experiment is low. The MVP is local-only, with no backend, no accounts, no third-party dependencies and no committed budget (FACT: PRODUCT-BRIEF.md "Situation"). The founder is also user zero.
- The goal → habit → task → progress loop is a coherent wedge (OPINION). It is not yet proven to be a differentiator.

Main risk:
- This is a crowded, low-willingness-to-pay category. Habit trackers, to-do apps and goal apps are all well established, and the brief contains no competitor analysis (FACT: absent from the brief). I have not researched competitors, so I can't say how many apps already link goals to habits and tasks (OPINION).
- Habit apps typically see steep early churn (OPINION, not sourced here).
- Many users already pay for or get free equivalents. Apple Reminders, Notion templates and existing habit apps are examples (OPINION).
- A solo founder gives no distribution advantage.
- Defensibility is weak. Anyone can copy the data model. Local-only storage also gives no switching cost or network effect, and the brief plans no sync (FACT: roadmap lists sync as deferred).

Key assumption:
- Enough people feel a real disconnect between their long-term goals and their daily actions to change tools and pay for a fix. Nothing in the brief tests this (FACT: "No users, no research, no revenue").
- A related assumption is that the connection, rather than the individual features, is what retains users.

Suggested improvement:
1. Reframe Phase 5 as a demand experiment, not only a personal-use test. Pre-define metrics such as day-14 and day-30 use, share of habits linked to goals, and whether goal-linking is used or skipped. Ben's own use is a single data point and biased.
2. Before Phase 6, recruit 5–10 target users to run a TestFlight beta. That needs Ben's approval and an Apple developer account, which is a cost. Alternatively, run cheap interviews or a landing-page test, which need no code.
3. Cut the MVP towards proving the loop. Candidates to defer are one-level subtasks, simple recurrence, N×-per-week schedules and JSON export. These are OPINION. Keep goals linked to habits with a progress view, since that is the differentiator.
4. Choose the primary user before launch. A narrow niche, such as students or people with a specific goal type, would beat the broad "anyone with goals" framing.
5. Don't build monetisation yet. Test two or three models later: a one-time purchase, a freemium split with unlimited goals as the paid tier, and a subscription. Subscription fatigue is a risk (OPINION).

Confidence: MEDIUM. The structural risks are clear, but I have no market data.

Evidence quality: WEAK. This rests on the brief and roadmap alone. I ran no competitor, pricing or market searches, and I have no user evidence.

Answers to my questions:
- **Why choose this over existing products?** Today there is no proven reason. The possible reason is a guilt-free, simple link between goals and daily actions. It is a hypothesis and needs comparison against the closest existing apps.
- **What could make them continue using it?** Seeing daily actions move a goal, a low-friction widget check-off, and non-punitive streaks. I have no evidence that any of these retain users.
- **What evidence would demonstrate genuine demand?** Unprompted retention at day 30 in non-friend testers, and users who say they'd be upset if the app went away. Unprompted organic installs or a waitlist would also count. Willingness to pay at a stated price is a further signal.
- **Which assumptions should be validated first?** In order:
  1. The problem is real for a defined user group.
  2. Goal-linking is used and not skipped.
  3. Users keep using it after the first month.
  4. Users will pay.
